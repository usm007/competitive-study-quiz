# Exam Study Notes: Fundamental Rights and the Brahmaputra System

## How to use these notes

These notes combine two high-yield exam areas: Indian Polity (Fundamental
Rights, Articles 12 to 35) and the Physical Geography of Assam with the
Brahmaputra river system. Read each section, memorise the definitions, learn
the distinctions, and test yourself with the tables and numbers at the end
of each section.

### 1. Fundamental Rights: Articles 12 to 35, an overview

The Constitution of India was adopted on 26 November 1949 and the
Fundamental Rights in Part III entered into force on 26 January 1950.
Fundamental Rights are contained in Articles 12 to 35 of the Constitution.
They are justiciable, which means a citizen can move the Supreme Court under
Article 32 or a High Court under Article 226 when a right is violated.

Definition: The term 'State' under Article 12 includes the Government and
Parliament of India, the Government and legislature of each State, all local
authorities, and all other authorities within the territory of India or under
the control of the Government of India.

Definition: 'Law' under Article 13 includes any ordinance, order, bye-law,
rule, regulation, notification, custom or usage having the force of law in
the territory of India.

Article 13 declares that all laws in force before 26 January 1950 that are
inconsistent with Fundamental Rights are void to the extent of the
inconsistency. It also bars the State from making any law that takes away or
abridges the rights conferred by Part III. The First Amendment of 1951 added
clause (4) to Article 13 protection for land-reform laws and inserted
Articles 31A and 31B with the Ninth Schedule. The Forty-second Amendment of
1976 attempted to shield all Directive Principles from Articles 14 and 19,
but the Forty-fourth Amendment of 1978 rolled back the worst excesses of the
Emergency and deleted the right to property as a Fundamental Right.

Key timeline points to memorise:

- 26 November 1949: the Constitution is adopted.
- 26 January 1950: Fundamental Rights come into force.
- 1951: the First Amendment adds Articles 31A, 31B and Article 15(4).
- 1976: the Forty-second Amendment inserts Articles 14A-related changes and
  the words Socialist, Secular and Integrity into the Preamble.
- 1978: the Forty-fourth Amendment deletes the right to property from
  Part III and makes Article 21 stronger against Emergency suspension.
- 1992: the Supreme Court decides Indra Sawhney and caps reservations at
  50 percent.
- 2002: the Eighty-sixth Amendment inserts Article 21A on free and
  compulsory education for children aged 6 to 14 years.
- 2009: the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act is
  passed to enforce Article 21A.
- 2015: the Supreme Court strikes down Section 66A of the Information
  Technology Act in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India.
- 2017: the nine-judge bench in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India
  declares privacy a Fundamental Right under Article 21.
- 2019: the One Hundred and Third Amendment inserts Articles 15(6) and
  16(6) providing up to 10 percent reservation for Economically Weaker
  Sections.

### 3. Article 14 and the equality code of Articles 15 and 16

Definition: 'Equality before the law' means the absence of any special
privilege for any person, while 'equal protection of the laws' means equals
must be treated equally and a reasonable classification of persons is
permitted.

Distinction: equality before the law is a negative concept that denies
special privilege, whereas equal protection of the laws is a positive concept
that permits reasonable classification of equals and unequals.

Article 14 covers all persons, citizens and non-citizens alike. A
classification under Article 14 is valid only if it rests on an intelligible
differentia and the differentia has a rational relation to the object of the
law. This two-part test is quoted in almost every equality judgment.

Distinction: Article 15 prohibits discrimination only on the grounds of
religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth, whereas Article 16 guarantees
equality of opportunity more broadly in matters of public employment.

Article 15 applies to both the State and, in some clauses, private
individuals running public places such as shops, wells, roads and places of
public resort. Article 16 applies to employment under the State. Article
15(3) permits special provisions for women and children, Article 15(4) added
in 1951 permits special provisions for socially and educationally backward
classes, and Articles 15(5) and 15(6) extend reservations to private
educational institutions and to Economically Weaker Sections respectively.
Article 16(4) permits reservation in favour of backward classes, Article
16(4A) permits reservation in promotion for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled
Tribes, and Article 16(6) inserted in 2019 permits up to 10 percent
reservation for Economically Weaker Sections.

### 4. Article 19: the six freedoms and reasonable restrictions

Article 19(1) guarantees six freedoms to citizens only, a point examiners
repeat every year. The six freedoms are:

- Freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a).
- Freedom to assemble peaceably and without arms under Article 19(1)(b).
- Freedom to form associations or unions under Article 19(1)(c).
- Freedom to move freely throughout India under Article 19(1)(d).
- Freedom to reside and settle in any part of India under Article
  19(1)(e).
- Freedom to practise any profession or carry on any occupation, trade or
  business under Article 19(1)(g). Note that the original clause (f) on
  property was removed by the Forty-fourth Amendment of 1978.

Each freedom is subject to reasonable restrictions in clauses (2) to (6),
including the sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State,
public order, decency, morality, contempt of court, defamation and
incitement to an offence. In 2015 the Supreme Court struck down Section 66A
of the Information Technology Act because vague limits on online speech were
not reasonable restrictions under Article 19(2).

Distinction: Article 19 protects the freedoms of citizens only, whereas
Article 21 protects the life and liberty of all persons including
non-citizens.

### 5. Article 21: life, liberty and an expanding promise

Article 21 states that no person shall be deprived of life or personal
liberty except according to procedure established by law. Before 1978 the
phrase was read narrowly, but in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India in 1978 the
Supreme Court held that the procedure must be fair, just and reasonable and
not arbitrary or oppressive.

Distinction: 'procedure established by law' as written in Article 21 means a
procedure laid down by a validly enacted law, whereas 'due process of law'
additionally demands that the law itself be fair and reasonable, and after
Maneka Gandhi in 1978 the Court reads Article 21 as including due-process
values.

Since 1978 the Court has read many implied rights into Article 21,
including the right to live with dignity, livelihood, shelter, health, a
clean environment, speedy trial, legal aid, and, in 2017, privacy in the
Puttaswamy judgment. Article 21A on education for children aged 6 to 14
years was inserted by the Eighty-sixth Amendment in 2002 and enforced
through the 2009 education Act.

Definition: 'Preventive detention' means detention of a person without trial
on the apprehension that the person may commit a future offence, whereas
punitive detention follows conviction for an offence already committed.

### 6. Article 32: the right to Constitutional remedies

Article 32 gives every citizen the right to move the Supreme Court for the
enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Dr. B. R. Ambedkar called Article 32 the
heart and soul of the Constitution. The Supreme Court has held that Article
32 itself is a Fundamental Right, so Parliament cannot suspend it by an
ordinary law.

The Court issues five writs under Articles 32 and 226:

- Habeas corpus: to produce a detained person before the court.
- Mandamus: to command a public authority to perform a legal duty.
- Prohibition: to restrain a lower court from exceeding its jurisdiction.
- Certiorari: to quash the order of a lower court or tribunal.
- Quo warranto: to question the authority by which a person holds a public
  office.

Definition: A 'writ' is a written order issued by a constitutional court
commanding a person or authority to do, or to refrain from doing, a
specified act.

Distinction: Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right and the Supreme Court
must grant a remedy when a Fundamental Right is violated, whereas Article
226 is wider in scope but discretionary, covering Fundamental Rights and
other legal rights before a High Court.

During the Emergency, the majority in ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla in
1976 held that Article 21 could be suspended; the Forty-fourth Amendment of
1978 then provided that Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended even during
an Emergency.

### 7. Physical geography of Assam and the Brahmaputra system

Assam lies in the eastern Himalayan foothills and the Brahmaputra valley.
The state covers about 78,438 square kilometres, and the Brahmaputra valley
is roughly 720 kilometres long from east to west and 80 to 100 kilometres
wide. The Brahmaputra is a braided river across most of Assam, shifting
channels, building sandbars called chars, and flooding heavily every
monsoon.

Definition: A 'braided river' is a wide, shallow river that splits into many
interlacing channels around temporary sandbars and islands.

Definition: 'Alluvium' is fertile silt, sand and clay deposited by rivers,
and the Brahmaputra valley floor is almost entirely recent alluvium.

The Brahmaputra rises as the Yarlung Tsangpo in Tibet near the Chemayungdung
glacier, flows about 2,900 kilometres in total, enters Arunachal Pradesh as
the Siang or Dihang, becomes the Brahmaputra in Assam after joining the
Dibang and Lohit at Kobo, and leaves Assam at Dhubri to enter Bangladesh as
the Jamuna. Within Assam the river flows for about 640 kilometres. Majuli,
formed between the Brahmaputra and the Subansiri-Luit channels, is among
the largest inhabited river islands in the world, though erosion has shrunk
it from over 1,200 square kilometres a century ago to roughly 350 to 500
square kilometres today.

The earthquake of 15 August 1950, measuring 8.6 in magnitude, tilted
tributary gradients, raised the river bed in places, and intensified floods
and bank erosion across Upper Assam. The Brahmaputra Board was set up in
1982 under the Brahmaputra Board Act of 1981 to plan flood control in the
valley.

### 8. Tributaries of the Brahmaputra in Assam

Distinction: north-bank tributaries rise in the Himalayas, carry heavy silt,
and build large alluvial fans, whereas south-bank tributaries rise in the
hills of Meghalaya, Nagaland and Arunachal Pradesh, carry less silt, and run
in comparatively stable, narrower channels.

The principal tributaries examiners expect are:

North-bank tributaries:

- Subansiri: rises in Tibet, joins at Lakhimpur; about 442 kilometres long.
- Ranganadi: rises in Arunachal Pradesh, joins near Lakhimpur.
- Jia Bharali (Kameng): rises in Arunachal Pradesh, joins at Tezpur.
- Puthimari: rises in Bhutan, joins near Kamrup.
- Manas: rises in Bhutan, joins near Jogighopa; a major transboundary
  river about 376 kilometres long.
- Sankosh (Gadadhar): forms part of the Assam-West Bengal boundary.

South-bank tributaries:

- Burhi Dihing: rises in the Patkai hills, joins near Dibrugarh.
- Disang: rises in Nagaland, joins near Sibsagar.
- Dikhow: rises in Nagaland, joins near Sibsagar.
- Dhansiri: rises in Nagaland, joins near Golaghat; about 352 kilometres
  long.
- Kopili: rises in Meghalaya, joins near Nagaon; about 256 kilometres
  long.
- Kulsi and Krishnai: rise in Meghalaya and join in Lower Assam.

### 9. Soils, climate and key numbers of Assam

Assam has three broad soil zones: recent alluvial soils of the flood
plains, old alluvial soils of the higher terraces, and laterite and red
soils on the Karbi and Dima Hasao hills with patches of forest soil.

Distinction: khadar soils are newer, finer, low-lying alluvium renewed by
almost every flood, whereas bhangar soils are older, coarser, higher-lying
alluvium above the normal flood limit.

The climate is humid subtropical with a strong monsoon. Average annual
rainfall for the state is about 2,300 millimetres, with Cherrapunji-Mawsynram
just across the border in Meghalaya receiving over 11,000 millimetres in
extreme years. Summer temperatures range from 30 to 35 degrees Celsius in
the valley, winter minimums fall to 6 to 10 degrees Celsius, and relative
humidity stays between 70 and 85 percent for much of the year. Roughly 75
percent of annual rain falls between June and September, and the state
records floods in most years: the devastating floods of 2022 affected all 35
districts and over 5.8 million people, while the 2011 census recorded Assam
at about 31.2 million people with a density near 398 persons per square
kilometre.

Quick numbers for revision:

- Total length of the Brahmaputra: about 2,900 kilometres.
- Length inside Assam: about 640 kilometres.
- Valley length by 720 kilometres east-west; width 80 to 100 kilometres.
- State area: about 78,438 square kilometres.
- Average rainfall: about 2,300 millimetres per year.
- Majuli island today: roughly 350 to 500 square kilometres.
- 1950 earthquake magnitude: 8.6 on 15 August 1950.
- Brahmaputra Board set up in 1982.
- Floods of 2022: over 5.8 million people affected across 35 districts.
- 2011 census: Assam population about 31.2 million.
